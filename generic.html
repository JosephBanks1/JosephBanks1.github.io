My Teaching Philosophy

Teaching Purpose

I didn't come to teaching through a classroom. I came to it through reconnaissance work — years spent reading people, building trust fast, and making sure information moved cleanly between people who didn't speak the same language, literally or otherwise. In HUMINT and OSINT work, a misunderstanding isn't an inconvenience — it costs something. That taught me to care about clarity in communication in a way most people don't.

I also taught myself to code with no formal training, no classroom, no instructor — just trial, error, and the discipline to keep going after failing at something publicly and privately. I know exactly how discouraging it is to sit alone with something you don't understand yet, and how much difference it makes to have someone who won't let you quit on yourself. That's the teacher I try to be.

I'm not going to pretend I got into ESL teaching chasing some lifelong calling — I got into it because knowledge is power. Teaching travels well and it pays, and that matters when you're watching an economy you trusted come apart in real time. But not romanticizing the job going in is exactly what makes me good at it: I show up focused on the actual outcome — did the student walk away able to do something they couldn't do before — instead of performing enthusiasm for its own sake.

Teaching Style

My style is closer to mission planning than lecture. Every lesson has a clear objective stated up front, a tight sequence to get there, and a short debrief at the end — that structure comes straight out of a decade in the military, where you don't move without knowing the objective and you don't finish without an after-action review. Students respond well to that. They know exactly what they're working toward and exactly how far they got.

Inside that structure, I run heavily Communicative — students are talking, not listening to me talk, as much as their level allows. Field work taught me that you learn a language by using it under pressure, not by studying it in isolation, so I build low-stakes pressure into the classroom deliberately: timed exchanges, role-play scenarios, put-yourself-in-the-situation prompts. It mirrors how I actually picked up skills that mattered — by being dropped into the situation and having to perform, with someone experienced there to correct me in real time.

Teaching Techniques

I open every lesson the way I'd open a briefing: here's what we're doing today, here's why it matters, here's what "done" looks like. Students aren't guessing about the plan.

For new material, I favor drill-and-apply over drill-and-drill: short, repeated practice of a structure, immediately followed by using it in something resembling a real exchange — ordering something, arguing a small point, giving directions, describing a problem.Role-play is central to how I teach speaking, and I run it more like an interview than a script: I set a scenario and a goal, and the student has to get from A to B using only what they've got, the way you'd have to in an actual conversation with a stranger. It's the same skill I spent years building in the field — figure out what the other person actually needs to hear to give you what you need, in real time, without a script.I close every lesson with a short after-action review: what worked, what didn't, what we're carrying into next time. Two minutes, not a lecture — but it keeps both of us honest about whether the lesson actually landed.

Also, I use several online platforms now, but previously I have used every medium available from sticks and mud to whiteboards and modern technology.
